# Tolkien Prose

Rewrite or compose in the narrative manner of J.R.R. Tolkien. The aim is a tale told as if remembered, not a ledger read aloud and not a modern essay in old clothes.

Read `references/examples.md` before writing.

## What the style actually is

Tolkien's English is mostly plain. The antique feeling comes from grammar, cadence, and the sense that the world is older than the page.

- Real archaic English is terse in word choice, not in sentence length. A paragraph may walk a long way on "and" before it rests. Do not confuse his economy of diction with a habit of three-word sentences.
- Archaism lives in word order and idiom. Inversion such as "Long have the halls lain empty" is truer than "eldritch," "ichor," or "tenebrous."
- Clauses often stand side by side, joined by "and," without a modern causal lecture.
- Register follows speaker and hour. A meal, a door, a twitch in a man's hand belong inside a longer sentence unless they change the hour.
- Landscape is seen from a standing place. Light, weather, and names do work that theme-statements must not do.
- Time has depth. What was, what remains, and what is forgotten are spoken as fact.
- People speak, but the tale is not a play. Most of a page should be walking narration. Speech comes at turns, not as the default vehicle for every fact.
- No two speakers share a mouth. Do not invent a full personality from a name and a class. If the user has not given voices, ask before writing much talk.

Letter 171 is the test. Genuine antique speech is tighter than modern speech. Slack or decorative idiom is the abuse. So is the opposite abuse: chopping a scene into a stack of isolated facts.

## Walking prose, not a tally

When the source is notes, a game log, or a list of discoveries, do not promote every bullet to its own sentence.

- Fold several related facts into one period so the reader moves through the room instead of stopping at each object.
- Give a sentence its own line only when the thing in it turns the scene (a name spoken in anger, a keyhole under the hand, a man falling).
- Skip matter that does not act. That a man is of average height, that there is construction in a house already under renovation, that someone looks tired, or that a party split to lose the watch may be left out or tucked into a clause. If it does not change what is done or feared, it is not a sentence.
- Do not write "His name was X" as a lone line after a description. Name him as he enters the action.
- Do not march "Then they went. Then they found. Then they asked." Vary the opening of periods. Let a place or a sound begin the sentence as often as a they.

Wrong (tally):

The door flew wide. An older man drove a watchman out. He was of common height. His right hand twitched. His name was Almaz Krole. Construction stood all about him.

Right (walking):

The door flew wide, and an older man, worn with years of work and with a twitch in his right hand, drove a watchman into the street.

‘About time,’ he said, noticing the assembled on his doorstep. ‘I asked for you days ago.’

This was the innkeeper known as Almaz Krole, and he drew them with a motion through the dust of the renovations into his office.

## How to write

1. Stay inside the scene. Tell what is seen, heard, done, remembered, and feared.
2. Prefer nouns of place, craft, kin, weather, and time over abstract nouns of interpretation.
3. Mix long walking sentences with short blows. The short ones must earn the stop.
4. Use alliteration only where the ear already wants it.
5. When rewriting a source, keep the facts that have force. Drop colourless inventory.
6. When talk is needed, write it in that person's mouth, not in a house style shared by the whole company. Lean toward narration. A long report may be told by the narrator with one or two spoken lines to give the voice. Do not turn a scene into a string of quotations.
7. If the notes contradict themselves, leave a name half-said, or skip a motive, mend the tale so a reader can follow it. Prefer the later or stronger fact. Do not advertise the mend.
8. After a draft, strike contrast formulas, forecasts, and any sentence that names a thing without seating it in action. Read the talk aloud. If two speakers could swap lines without anyone noticing, rewrite until they could not.

## Forbidden habits

### Contrast formulas

Do not write "It is not X. It is Y." Do not write "not a ledger but a story," "not merely," or "this is no ordinary." If a contrast was never in play, do not import it.

### Unmotivated should and looking ahead

Do not add "an inn that should not have been haunted" or "they have not yet reached their destination." Show strangeness. Do not advertise remaining chapters.

### Play-script and vanished narration

Do not let a scene become almost nothing but dialogue. The company walking a room, a print on plaster, the twitch of a hand, the washed marble — these belong in the narrator's period, not in another spoken line.

Wrong: four speakers taking turns to announce each discovery.

Right: the narrator carries the seeing and the going. A person speaks when they cut, dodge, boast, or name what the others will not.

Reported speech is allowed for the bulk of a tale told in-scene (Krole's account of the three dead may be narrated). Keep a scrap of his own wording so the mouth is still his.

Keep tags plain (said, answered, muttered, asked). Do not dummy-thee the whole company.

### All talk and no road

If more than about a third of a scene is inside quotation marks, cut lines and give the facts back to the narrator. Questions that only prompt the next fact ("And then?"; "What about Callie?") can usually go.

## Distinct mouths

Follow Tolkien's own account, not a modern list of quirks.

In Appendix F ("On Translation") he says the Common Speech has been turned into modern English, and that in doing so the varieties of that speech have been lessened. Some attempt was made to show them by variations in the kind of English used. Hobbits spoke for the most part a rustic dialect; in Gondor and Rohan a more antique language was used, more formal and more terse. The real divergence of idiom was greater than the book shows. To mark it fully would have meant phonetic Shire-spellings and an archaic Gondor that would have puzzled or infuriated readers.

In Letter 193 (to the BBC man who wanted "accents") he says that if the history were real, every speaker would give away place, people, and rank by accent, but that cannot be represented when Common Speech is turned into English, and is not necessary. He had already paid great attention to such differentiation as was possible: in diction, idiom, and so on. He told them not to hang dropped aitches on Orcs; that was not in the text, and he had left it out on purpose. Minas Tirith he compared to London: no special accent laid over the speech. Rohan might be slower and more sonorous, a nicety he said could be neglected on the air.

Letter 171 adds the rest. A king does not think modern thoughts in slack modern idiom. The wealth of English offers more than one style. There is no more reason to flatten Théoden into one contemporary voice than to change his hauberk into a uniform.

So the work is variation of English by people, place, rank, and schooling. It is not stage-accent, phonetic spelling, or a "personality pack" of pet words and signature laughs. A true idiolect (Gollum's we and I, Sam's rustic hedges) appears only where the source has already marked one. Tolkien often uses a register to enact a shared stance (hobbit, captain, elf) rather than a unique brand for every name. The Fellowship still do not share a mouth: inversion and memory in one, colloquial invention in another, questions in a third. That is diction and idiom.

### Ask first

Session notes record deeds. They rarely record people, rank, or schooling, which are what he used. If those are missing for anyone who will speak more than a line or two, ask before writing much talk.

Ask only what that method needs:

- people / homeland / tongue
- rank and trade
- schooling or none
- rustic or antique, formal or familiar (the Appendix F split)
- any marked idiom already in the log (an oath, a gunmage's shortness, a stall-keeper's patter)

Do not ask for a pile of tics. One line is enough. "Faye is Iosan, a fighter, short in talk" maps to terse familiar English. "Lysander is church-trained and pleased with a phrase" maps to a more formal line.

If the user says to proceed without that, keep speech scarce and close to the narrator until a difference of people or rank has been earned by the log.

### Use what you are given

A spoken line must fit people and rank. If two speakers of different rank could swap it, it is unmarked Common Speech, and should be rarer than it looks. Do not mark the difference with phonetic spelling, dropped letters, or "she said impatiently." The wording is the mark. A man may change register when the hour changes. What is not allowed is one house style.

## Mending the notes

- If an early report is disproved later (e.g. a captain "lost at sea" who tells them he was killed in a tavern), write the first as rumour and the second as the man's own word.
- You may supply a look, a habit, a reason for silence, or the exact words of a talk the notes only summarise as long as it makes sense in the scene.
- Do not add a new villain, a new gate, or a victory or defeat that did not happen.

### Telegram chronicle

Do not render a series of notes as one short sentence per bullet. That is the failure this skill is most likely to commit when the source is notes. If a paragraph could be replaced by the original bullet list, it should not be done.

### Decorative gloom and amateur medievalism

Do not sprinkle unearned doom. Do not use "behold" except at a true turning. Avoid costume words and dummy-thee-ing.

## Rewrite method

- First pass. If recurring speakers lack cards and you cannot infer the cards from the content, ask for them. Then read the notes as scenes, not as items to check off.
- Second pass. Write walking narration first. Add speech only at turns.
- Third pass. Cut contrast formulas, forecasts, prompt-questions, and any line of talk that only restates what the narrator already showed.
- Read aloud. If you hear a list, join it. If two speakers could swap lines, rewrite. If the scene is mostly quotation marks, give the road back to the narrator.
